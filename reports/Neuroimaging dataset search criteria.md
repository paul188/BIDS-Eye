# Modality, population and task anchor neuroimaging dataset searches

People who look for open neuroimaging data filter first on **modality** (93% of the 57 papers and forum requests coded here), then on **task or paradigm, including resting state** (46%), and on **population or diagnosis** (42%). Close behind come **open access** (mostly assumed rather than checked), **preprocessing or derivative state**, **acquisition parameters** such as field strength, TR and sequence, and **minimum sample size**. Two groups behave differently. Methods and machine-learning builders, who publish most of the multi-dataset pooling papers, filter on technical and legal properties: scan pairs within the same session, TR and voxel thresholds, BIDS validity, and licence clauses. Topic-driven scientists and students filter on condition and paradigm. The demographic facets that repositories offer most often are age, sex, species and date, and they are the criteria people state least often: **age 4/57, sex 1/57, species 1/57, site or institution 0/57**. Meanwhile, the criteria people do state (stimulus content, design structure, preprocessing pipeline, sidecar parameters, phenotype instruments) are either indexed nowhere or exist only as free text. The evidence base is thin. No published query log or user study covers any neuroimaging repository, so the ranking below comes from a 14-paper verified sample, a 43-request forum corpus, 12 feature requests, and the facet code of seven systems. It still points to a clear design for the BIDS-Eye criteria registry. For every criterion, the registry should record whether the evidence comes from BIDS files, a curator's free-text field, or README and paper prose. It should say whether the constraint binds to one participant or to the whole dataset. And it should show "not recorded" to the user instead of silently dropping the dataset, which is what every surveyed portal except CONP does today.

## No one has logged how neuroimaging researchers search, so four proxies must stand in

There is **no published survey, interview study or query-log analysis of how neuroimaging researchers discover datasets**. The neuroimaging surveys that do exist cover data management and sharing: Borghi & Van Gulick's 144 MRI researchers ([PLOS ONE 2018](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC6047789)) and Paret et al.'s 283 fMRI authors ([NeuroImage 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2022.119306)). Neither asks how people choose a dataset to reuse. OpenNeuro, NDA and DANDI have not published their query logs. What OpenNeuro's authors did publish is a measure of reuse: **165 papers reused 111 OpenNeuro datasets**, and **31 of those papers pooled two or more datasets, up to 40** ([Markiewicz et al. 2021, eLife](https://elifesciences.org/articles/71774)). So most reuse is known-item retrieval of a single accession number, while a significant minority is recall-oriented "find every dataset with property X" aggregation. The second kind is where explicit multi-criteria filtering happens.

This report therefore triangulates four proxies. The first is a verified sample of **14 papers that state dataset-level selection criteria**, drawn from a Europe PMC full-text harvest of 341 candidates. The second is **43 verbatim dataset requests** from NeuroStars, Stack Exchange, the MNE forum and GitHub, dated 2013–2025, plus **12 "meta" requests** asking for new search capabilities. The third is the facet and schema code of **OpenNeuro, Neurobagel, DANDI, EBRAINS/openMINDS, CONP and NeuroVault**, verified in source on 2026-11-14, together with documentation for NDA, NITRC-IR, LONI IDA, COINS and XNAT. The fourth is cross-domain dataset-search research. All four are small or indirect. The paper and forum tallies were each hand-coded by one coder. Reddit could not be reached. Brain-age and normative-modelling pooling papers, which probably lean on age and health status, could not be read in full. The percentages below should be read as rank-order evidence, not as precise rates.

The cross-domain studies matter because they describe the shape of a dataset query. On the European Data Portal, queries had **mode and median length of one word and a mean near 1.5**, and the authors concluded that users treat "the search box in a similar way to a facet" ([EDP Analytical Report 18](https://data.europa.eu/sites/default/files/analytical_report_18-characterising_data_search_edp.pdf)). When people describe their needs freely, the queries change: crowdsourced dataset descriptions were "much longer" and held **seven times more geospatial and temporal mentions** ([Kacprzak et al. 2018](https://eprints.cs.univie.ac.at/7688)). Koesten's interviews explain the gap: users "do not expect that the search functionality will be able to provide relevant data for longer and more specific queries" ([EDP Report 18](https://data.europa.eu/sites/default/files/analytical_report_18-characterising_data_search_edp.pdf)). Short queries reflect low expectations as much as simple needs, and a natural-language engine can draw out the longer form. Dataset queries also contain "significantly more numerical digits" than publication queries (Carevic et al., via [EDP Report 18](https://data.europa.eu/sites/default/files/analytical_report_18-characterising_data_search_edp.pdf)). In neuroimaging that numeric pattern recurs as thresholds on N, age, TR and field strength.

The largest survey of what drives data selection, Gregory et al.'s **1,677 respondents**, ranks **"data collection conditions and methodology" first (89% important or extremely important)**, followed by processing and handling information and topical relevance, then **ease of access (73%)**, source reputation (71%) and creator reputation (62%) ([Gregory et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.00464)). The same study found that **literature is the usual gateway to data**: respondents typically "first find the literature, and then use the literature as a gateway to locating the data". One respondent described data search as "specifying parameters e.g. geographical and date coverage, or by looking for data created by a specific organization" ([Gregory et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.00464)). In biomedicine, bioCADDIE's 30 retained competency questions were dominated by **data type, disease, biological process and organism**, with **permissions, data format and quality proxies (funding source, number of referring publications)** behind them ([Cohen et al. 2017](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC5737202)). Those four map directly onto BIDS modality, diagnosis, task or construct, and species. Even with full metadata in hand, bioCADDIE judges "often looked beyond metadata to linked PubMed articles", and **81% of pooled candidates were not relevant**, which shows how hard multi-constraint precision is when metadata is terse ([Cohen et al. 2017](https://academic.oup.com/database/article/4085942)).

Three tiers stand out. **Tier 1 (modality, task, population)** appears in at least four in ten statements and matches bioCADDIE's top competency entities, so it is the core of almost every query. Modality is close to universal, but on its own it rarely separates good results from bad ones. The forum modality mix was fMRI 20, EEG 11, structural or other MRI 9, DWI 2, fNIRS 2, CT 1, and MEG, PET and iEEG zero ([forum corpus, e.g. NeuroStars](https://neurostars.org/t/eeg-datasets-for-autism-control/1372)). The zero for MEG, PET and iEEG probably reflects which forums were sampled, not a lack of demand. Within the task criterion, **resting state is the single most requested "task"** (8 of 43). People treat it as a modality-level term ("rs-fMRI", "resting-state EEG"), so the registry should make it a first-class value and not only a BIDS `task-rest` label.

**Tier 2 (access, derivatives, acquisition, sample size, quality)** splits by user type. Students and ML practitioners ask for "preprocessed", "labelled" or "ML-ready" data and treat diagnosis as a class label ([N17](https://neurostars.org/t/need-preprocessed-fmri-dataset/23131), [S13](https://opendata.stackexchange.com/questions/18515/can-i-find-a-dataset-of-eeg-signals-to-classify-a-depression)). Tool builders ask for TR, voxel and field-strength thresholds and for integrity. "Open/public" appears in half the forum requests but works as a **default assumption, not a discriminating filter**. Licence becomes a real constraint only when the user plans to redistribute or build a commercial ML product: "I need the images to be legally open source so that I'm not distributing images that have copy right restrictions" ([S9](https://opendata.stackexchange.com/questions/6661/open-source-mri-image-dataset)), and EEGDash excluded "small brain–computer-interface sets under no-derivatives licences, which forbid a converted copy" ([Shahi et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.06356v1)). Portal logs agree. On the European Data Portal, "format and licence are the least popular" facets ([EDP Report 18](https://data.europa.eu/sites/default/files/analytical_report_18-characterising_data_search_edp.pdf)), yet Gregory's respondents named inaccessibility as the **top discovery barrier (68%)** ([Gregory et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.00464)). Licence should be shown on every result, but it is rarely what people search for.

**Tier 3 holds the demographic and provenance facets that portals expose and requesters rarely mention.** This is the central paradox of the catalogue. OpenNeuro, Neurobagel, NITRC-IR, COINS and IDA all filter on age, and four systems filter on sex. Yet age appeared in 4 of 57 statements and sex in 1. The one forum request that used both was "an adult man healthy/normal EEG Relative Power Spectra Density during rest" ([S14](https://opendata.stackexchange.com/questions/18518/human-normal-eeg-relative-power-spectra-during-rest)). The paradox has three plausible explanations, and the evidence cannot separate them. First, demographics act as **subject-level filters applied after a dataset is chosen**, as in "After excluding 18 participants without the required baseline age or sex information" ([Li et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fneur.2026.1964745)). Second, the paper sample under-represents brain-age and lifespan studies. Third, people who do want demographic filtering stop writing free-text requests and go straight to tools. The meta-requests support the third reading: power users asked to filter by "age, scan type, fieldmaps … male 40yo" and to "list all manufacturers on openneuro" ([X3](https://neurostars.org/t/datalad-search-openneuro/18386)). Species is a special case. It is silent rather than unimportant, because human is assumed everywhere and BIDS states that a missing species means *Homo sapiens* ([BIDS spec](https://bids-specification.readthedocs.io/en/stable/modality-agnostic-files/data-summary-files.html)). Animal researchers do ask, for example for non-human primates ([OpenNeuro #3904](https://github.com/OpenNeuroOrg/openneuro/issues/3904)), and organism is one of bioCADDIE's top four. **Site, institution and country never appeared** as a selection criterion in papers or forum posts. That contrasts sharply with government-data portals, where (Country, Category) was the most-used facet pair ([EDP Report 18](https://data.europa.eu/sites/default/files/analytical_report_18-characterising_data_search_edp.pdf)). The only neuroimaging-adjacent evidence for institution search is Gregory's "data created by a specific organization" quote and the multisite-homogeneity criterion in Isherwood's review ([Isherwood et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0248341)).

## Requests arrive as two-constraint sentences, numeric thresholds and explicit exclusions

The typical forum request has **two or three constraints: modality plus diagnosis or rest/task, plus "open" or "preprocessed"**. Examples: "Anyone know of any publicly available EEG data for subject with autism?" ([N4](https://neurostars.org/t/publicly-available-eeg-data-in-autism/5224)); "does somebody know about a dataset of Epilepsy patients with resting-state fMRI?" ([N9](https://neurostars.org/t/is-there-an-available-open-rs-fmri-epilepsy-dataset/6716)); "where can i find preprocessed fmri data set for alzheimer's disease" ([S3](https://psychology.stackexchange.com/questions/16824/where-can-i-find-preprocessed-fmri-data-set-for-alzheimers-disease)). Diagnosis is often phrased as a contrast, which carries the implicit requirement that controls are present: "open eeg datasets for autism subjects vs control similar to them fmri datasets like abide" ([N2](https://neurostars.org/t/eeg-datasets-for-autism-control/1372)) and "EEG data of normal patients as well as those suffering from Alzheimer's disease" ([N13](https://neurostars.org/t/can-someone-tell-me-how-can-i-find-eeg-data-of-normal-alzheimer-patients/14104)). Non-specialists also confuse labels, as in "Healthy Patients" ([N6](https://neurostars.org/t/where-to-find-healthy-patients-dataset-of-fmri-in-adni-database-and-preprocessing-steps-of-resting-state-fmri-data/5858)). The diagnosis vocabulary in requests spans autism, Alzheimer's (EEG, fMRI and fNIRS), schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, epilepsy, brain tumour subtypes, depression, Covid-19 and disorders of consciousness. Most of these fall into "Other" in OpenNeuro's five-item diagnosis list ([OpenNeuro facets](https://github.com/OpenNeuroOrg/openneuro/blob/master/packages/openneuro-app/src/scripts/search/initial-search-params.tsx)).

Methods developers write longer specifications full of **numeric comparisons**. In papers: "each dataset that contained at least five T1w MRIs from at least five adult HCs" and "in the age range from 2 to 12 years" ([Fisch et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43588-026-00953-7)); "Having >12 subjects … EPI: TR > 1.5 s, all voxel edges < 4.1 mm, number of volumes > 100" ([Taylor et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2023.1205928)); "Includes at least one subject between 5 and 21. Field strength should be three Tesla" ([Bricout et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2026.1817743)); "Recordings of 10 seconds or less, or sampled below 200 Hz, are skipped" ([Shahi et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.06356v1)). In forums: "fast TR, ideally lower than 1.0 and ideally a range of TRs" ([N5](https://neurostars.org/t/fast-tr-open-task-fmri-datasets/5601)); "at least 20 Participants and 30 trials per task" ([S4](https://psychology.stackexchange.com/questions/19500/task-based-fmri-dataset-with-very-simple-task-e-g-view-stripes)); "low-field MRI (<1T is already lowfield for me) paired to something at least 3T" ([N20](https://neurostars.org/t/dataset-lowfield-mri/28588)). The registry therefore needs **typed, unit-aware comparison operators** on counts, ages and acquisition values, and **subgroup counts**, because "five adult healthy controls" is a count over a filtered subset of participants, not over the whole dataset.

Users also state **exclusions** and **co-occurrence constraints**, and a flat facet cannot express either. Exclusions read like "with several sessions per subject … no RS" ([N7](https://neurostars.org/t/inter-subject-decoding-datasets/5965)), "acquired using MP-RAGE (not MP2-RAGE)" ([S2](https://psychology.stackexchange.com/questions/12988/open-access-7t-resting-state-fmri-dataset)), and "No prospective motion correction techniques (e.g., vNavs)" ([Bricout et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2026.1817743)). Co-occurrence constraints read like "only datasets including at least one T1-weighted (T1w) and one BOLD per subject run were included" ([Esteban et al. 2019](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC6319393)), "at least one EPI and one T1w volume in the same session directory" ([Taylor et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2023.1205928)), and "both task fMRI langauge and DTI data" ([N16](https://neurostars.org/t/language-fmri-task-public-datasets/22405)). The same co-occurrence problem affects participant demographics. OpenNeuro's age and sex filters run separate queries over participant rows but return datasets, so **"female AND aged 60–70" matches any dataset with some female and some 60–70-year-old, not necessarily the same person**. Neurobagel avoids this by binding every condition to one phenotypic session ([OpenNeuro build-search-query](https://github.com/OpenNeuroOrg/openneuro/blob/master/packages/openneuro-server/src/graphql/resolvers/build-search-query.ts); [Neurobagel utility.py](https://github.com/neurobagel/api/blob/main/app/api/utility.py)).

Two further phrasing patterns deserve explicit handling. The first is the **known-dataset anchor**, which appears in 6 of 43 requests: "like abide", "I'm currently using HCP data" ([N16](https://neurostars.org/t/language-fmri-task-public-datasets/22405)), "For now the one I'm mostly using are Individual Brain Charting ; BOLD5000 ; Forrest" ([N7](https://neurostars.org/t/inter-subject-decoding-datasets/5965)). Together with Gregory's "literature as gateway" finding, this argues for dataset-to-dataset similarity and for matching on paper titles, DOIs and author names. The second is **stimulus content and design structure**, which no structured field captures: "viewing natural images (maybe preferably single object)" ([N12](https://neurostars.org/t/looking-for-public-fmri-datasets-viewing-natural-images/13543)), "videos of people talking … The video should include the face of the speaker" ([N18](https://neurostars.org/t/fmri-dataset-of-people-watching-others-talking/28089)), "'rest-task-rest' design (scanned in one day)" ([N11](https://neurostars.org/t/cant-find-an-open-fmri-dataset-with-rest-task-rest-design/7207)). Paper authors face the same gap with topic fit: "alignment with the consolidation clusters" ([Bartzioka & Khosravi 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ynirp.2026.100398)), "experiments presenting isolated words" ([Denissen et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-024-04282-0)), and studies "that reported clearly defined regions of brain activation", which took **six months of vetting associated publications** ([Bowring et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1002/hbm.24603)). About a third of forum requests include at least one such constraint that is not in BIDS metadata.

Users repeatedly say that existing search failed them: "I already searched through OpenNeuro and don't find anymore" ([N7](https://neurostars.org/t/inter-subject-decoding-datasets/5965)); "I'm not sure if I'm searching improperly" ([N22](https://neurostars.org/t/looking-for-test-fmri-data-with-dir-ap-and-dir-pa-scans/29465)); "I've been searching forever and haven't even come close" ([S4](https://psychology.stackexchange.com/questions/19500/task-based-fmri-dataset-with-very-simple-task-e-g-view-stripes)). OpenNeuro's own maintainer conceded that exact keyword matching does not "correspond to how people actually search": someone searching for EEG expects sEEG results, and "M/EEG" should find both MEG and EEG ([OpenNeuro #2586](https://github.com/OpenNeuroOrg/openneuro/issues/2586)). Other recorded failures are concrete vocabulary mismatches. The sex filter missed datasets coded "M"/"F" ([#2921](https://github.com/OpenNeuroOrg/openneuro/issues/2921)), and the query "spinal" did not find a dataset titled "Spinal Cord MRI Public Database" ([X2](https://neurostars.org/t/dysfunctional-search-feature-in-openneuro-website/4983)).

## Evidence lives in three stores of very different reliability

Each criterion is answered from one of three evidence stores, and they differ in fill rate and trustworthiness. The **BIDS-derived store** covers file tree, suffixes, task labels, participants.tsv and sidecar JSON. It answers modality, task label, subject and session counts, age, sex, species and acquisition parameters, and it is well populated wherever BIDS is followed. The **curator free-text store** is OpenNeuro's separate Metadata record, whose fields (dxStatus, species, studyLongitudinal, studyDomain, associatedPaperDOI, seniorAuthor, grantFunderName) are all plain strings that can hold "'true' | 'false' | 'user input string'" ([metadata.ts](https://github.com/OpenNeuroOrg/openneuro/blob/master/packages/openneuro-server/src/models/metadata.ts)). That store drives OpenNeuro's diagnosis, species, longitudinal and domain facets, and it is sparse. Back in 2019 the OpenNeuro team kept an external spreadsheet of subject counts, modalities, diagnosis and species because it "supports complex queries that are not currently supported by the site's search functionality" ([Stanford reproducibility blog](https://reproducibility.stanford.edu/index.html@p=921.html)). The **prose and data store** (README, linked paper, events files, raw image content) is the only source for stimulus content, design, topic fit, quality and affiliations. OpenNeuro's own authors mined Cognitive Atlas terms from "titles and README files" to describe their corpus, and they acknowledged that "a limited amount of dataset-level metadata is collected beyond that present within the BIDS metadata" ([Markiewicz et al. 2021](https://elifesciences.org/articles/71774)). Reusers report gaps such as "the precise sampling frequency and preprocessing steps … are not explicitly documented in the OpenNeuro entry" ([Esteve et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-23686-5)). They also report participant exclusions that only release notes and events files reveal ([Gao & Diniz 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10548-026-01238-y)).

Controlled vocabularies exist for most criteria, but **no verified system expands a parent term to its children at query time**. Neurobagel binds sex, diagnosis and assessments to SNOMED CT, healthy control to NCIt, modality to NIDM and pipelines to the Nipoppy catalog ([Neurobagel term standards](https://neurobagel.org/data_models/term_naming_standards/)). It matches exact CURIEs and ANDs multiple diagnoses. EBRAINS declares all facets `isHierarchical: false` ([KG settings](https://search.kg.ebrains.eu/api/settings)). DANDI stores species, anatomy (UBERON), disorders (SNOMED or Biolink), approach and technique as identifier-plus-name pairs and matches names by regex ([dandischema](https://github.com/dandi/dandi-schema/blob/master/dandischema/models.py)). NeuroVault links paradigms to Cognitive Atlas, but reusers found labels "not labeled in a standardized way" ([Germani et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/gigascience/giad029)), and a NeuroVault user reported that the Cognitive Atlas fields "are not filled by most users" ([Neurostars](https://neurostars.org/t/accessing-collection-metadata-via-neurovault-website/5736)). For institutions, only openMINDS models a hierarchy (`Organization.hasParent`, persistent identifiers, country of formation) ([openMINDS organization](https://github.com/openMetadataInitiative/openMINDS_core/blob/main/schemas/actors/organization.schema.tpl.json)). DANDI stores ROR identifiers on affiliations but offers no operator to search them. CONP reduces origin to "Canadian vs International" ([CONP dataset_list.py](https://github.com/CONP-PCNO/conp-portal/blob/master/app/search/dataset_list.py)). The only documented ontology-based query expansion is pre-LLM: DataMed's UMLS/terminology expansion and dkNET/NIF's NIFSTD synonyms ([DataMed](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7378878); [SciCrunch](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-1327/icbo2014_paper_36.pdf)). Existing LLM front ends, such as Neurobagel's query-tool-ai, translate text into the backend's existing API and inherit its vocabulary and missing-data limits ([query-tool-ai](https://github.com/neurobagel/query-tool-ai)).

### A registry specification, criterion by criterion

## Portals silently drop unrecorded datasets; the registry should not

Every verified system except one **silently excludes datasets that lack a value when a filter on that field is applied**. OpenNeuro's subject-count script returns false on an empty `summary.subjects`, and its age and sex range queries cannot match datasets with no participants.tsv values ([build-search-query.ts](https://github.com/OpenNeuroOrg/openneuro/blob/master/packages/openneuro-server/src/graphql/resolvers/build-search-query.ts)). Neurobagel wraps optional properties in SPARQL `OPTIONAL` for display but enforces filters as required triples, so a participant without a recorded age disappears from an age query ([Neurobagel utility.py](https://github.com/neurobagel/api/blob/main/app/api/utility.py)). COINS returns "results for any study for which the attribute is available" ([COINS help](https://coins.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/204266439-Browse-Available-Data)). Only CONP shows absence to users, as an "Access: Not stated" bucket, after stripping placeholders such as `n/a`, `unknown` and `not specified` ([CONP dataset_list.py](https://github.com/CONP-PCNO/conp-portal/blob/master/app/search/dataset_list.py)). The evidence supports keeping three states apart. **Not recorded** means nothing is known. **Explicitly missing** is BIDS `n/a` ([BIDS spec](https://bids-specification.readthedocs.io/en/stable/modality-agnostic-files/data-summary-files.html)) or a Neurobagel `MissingValues` entry ([Neurobagel dictionaries](https://neurobagel.org/data_models/dictionaries/)). **Withheld** covers Neurobagel's "protected" aggregate-only nodes ([Neurobagel query tool](https://neurobagel.org/user_guide/query_tool/)). A fourth case is a **defaulted** value, such as BIDS's human-species default, which should be labelled as an assumption rather than an observation.

The opposite failure, false matches, is just as well documented. Free-text keyword search returned datasets with "no relationship with language" ([Rampinini et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-024-04357-y)). bioCADDIE's relevance rule offers a usable fix: a record is relevant only when it "has all key concepts and answers the question", and it is not relevant when it has fewer than half ([Teodoro et al. 2017](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC5714153)). Checking each criterion separately against its own evidence store, as the registry proposes, puts that rule into practice. Doing so requires knowing **which mentions are evidence about the dataset and which are context**: a disease named as motivation, a cited lab, a parameter from another study, or a pipeline the authors ran but did not share. LLM annotation of neuroimaging publications reaches **0.91–0.97 accuracy zero-shot**, with errors comparable to human annotators ([Frontiers in Neuroinformatics 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12405296/)). That makes extracting README and paper evidence for each criterion feasible, provided the extraction records which store each value came from.

## Conclusion

The ranking shows that people and portals disagree about what dataset search is for. Portals were built around demographics and dates, which are easy to compute from structured files. People search by paradigm, condition, preprocessing state and acquisition physics, which mostly live in prose, sidecars and file trees. A criteria registry closes that gap if every criterion carries five properties: an evidence store with a reliability grade (BIDS file, curator string, or prose), a scope (participant, session, subject or dataset), a typed operator (category with subsumption, numeric with units, presence, co-occurrence, exclusion), a missing-value policy that distinguishes unrecorded, `n/a`, withheld and defaulted, and a list of non-evidential mention contexts. The decisive capabilities are joint participant-scoped constraints, same-session co-occurrence, numeric thresholds on sidecar parameters, and showing "unknown" instead of dropping the dataset. No surveyed repository or LLM front end provides all four.

Two open questions matter most for prioritising the work. First, the low demand for age, sex and institution may be an artefact of who posts on forums and which papers could be read. A metadata-coverage audit of OpenNeuro, combined with BIDS-Eye's own query logs once available, would settle whether these are rare needs or unmet ones. Second, because no neuroimaging query log has ever been published, BIDS-Eye's logs, coded against this catalogue, would be the first direct evidence of how researchers phrase dataset needs when the interface invites full sentences instead of one-word facet substitutes.
